There is a moment I keep coming back to. A night shift on a research vessel somewhere in the North Atlantic, hauling a trawl net up from the dark. And in it: an anglerfish. A real one, from the deep.

It was small, barely the length of my hand, but unmistakable — that alien silhouette, the dangling lure still intact, the enormous jaw. I held it for a moment before putting it in a sample bag. Then I took a tissue biopsy and stored it in a cryotube, not entirely sure what I would eventually do with it.

That encounter is part of why I am building this website, and why I am applying for funding to pursue the research that keeps me awake at night.

---

## What I study

My research sits at the intersection of marine biology, evolutionary genomics, and bioinformatics. The central question is deceptively simple: **how do fish survive in the deep sea?**

The deep ocean is one of the most extreme environments on Earth. Crushing pressure. Near-freezing temperatures. Complete, absolute darkness. And yet it is full of life — much of it still undescribed, let alone genomically characterised.

Most genomic studies of adaptation focus on protein-coding genes — the ~2% of the genome that directly encodes proteins. But I am increasingly convinced that the other 98%, the so-called "dark genome" of non-coding regulatory DNA, is where a large part of the story is hidden.

---

## Why the dark genome?

Non-coding DNA was once dismissed as "junk." We now know it is anything but. It contains regulatory elements that control *when* and *where* genes are switched on, and changes in these regions can drive major evolutionary transitions without altering a single protein.

Studies in birds and lizards have already shown how non-coding elements can drive phenotypic evolution. My hypothesis is that the same is true in deep-sea and Arctic fish — and that by looking at this neglected portion of the genome, we will find convergent adaptations that no one has described before.

*Convergent* is the key word. Across the tree of life, unrelated lineages have independently evolved similar solutions to similar problems — antifreeze proteins in polar fish, pressure-stable enzymes in hadal amphipods. I want to know whether the same thing happens at the regulatory level: do unrelated deep-sea fish arrive at the same genomic solutions independently?
