## What I study

My research sits at the intersection of marine biology, evolutionary genomics, and bioinformatics. The central question is deceptively simple: **how do deep-sea fish survive?**

The deep ocean is one of the most extreme environments on Earth. Crushing pressure. Near-freezing temperatures. Complete, absolute darkness. And yet, life thrives there in astonishing diversity.

Most genomic studies of adaptation focus on protein-coding genes — the ~2% of the genome that directly encodes proteins. But I am increasingly convinced that the other 98%, the so-called "dark genome" of non-coding regulatory DNA, is where a large part of the story is hidden.

## Why the dark genome?

Non-coding DNA was once dismissed as "junk." We now know it is anything but. It contains regulatory elements that control *when* and *where* genes are switched on and off. Changes in these regions can drive major evolutionary transitions without altering a single protein.

Studies in birds and lizards have already shown how non-coding elements can drive phenotypic evolution. My hypothesis is that the same is true in deep-sea fish — and that by looking at this neglected portion of the genome, we will find convergent adaptations that no one has described before.

*Convergent* is the key word. Across the tree of life, unrelated lineages have independently evolved similar solutions to similar problems — antifreeze proteins in fish and insects, pressure-stable enzymes in hadal amphipods. I want to know whether the same thing happens at the regulatory level: do unrelated deep-sea fish and cephalopods evolve the same genomic solutions independently?
